Nobody spends their whole income on food. Rent, fuel, soap and transport come first. Across poor households roughly 52 cents in every dollar is what is left for food — so the price on the shelf and the wage you need to pay it are two different numbers. Where the data comes from

Cost and affordability of a healthy diet

Food Prices for Nutrition 5.0, World Bank, released July 2026. Diet cost per person per day in local currency and 2021 PPP dollars, the cost and share of each food group, the ratio of diet cost to the food component of the international poverty line, and the published share and number of people unable to afford a healthy diet, 2017–2025. Background methodology at the Food Prices for Nutrition DataHub. Licensed CC BY 4.0.

Incomes and inequality

Poverty and Inequality Platform, World Bank. Household survey mean welfare, Gini coefficients and distribution estimates for 172 economies in 2021 PPP dollars. Whether a country reports consumption or income, and the year of its most recent survey, are shown alongside every figure on the affordability page.

Country context

World Development Indicators, World Bank, for GDP per capita, urban and rural shares, agricultural land, employment and value added, exchange rates and the food production index. Food Balance Sheets, FAOSTAT, for production, imports, exports and domestic supply. Region and income-group labels follow the World Bank classification.

What is measured and what we assumed

Taken straight from the source, unchanged

The daily cost of a healthy diet and its split across the six food groups. The published share and number of people unable to afford a healthy diet — the headline percentage on page one is the official figure, not our estimate. The diet cost as a multiple of the poverty line. Every survey mean, Gini and World Development Indicator.

A healthy diet is defined by Food Prices for Nutrition as the least expensive locally available foods meeting national dietary guidelines for one adult at 2,330 kcal a day. It is a price benchmark, not a record of what anyone eats.

Our own steps, and why

The 52 percent food share. The income figure on page one is the diet cost divided by 0.52 — the share of income low-income households have available for food, and the basis of the affordability measure in the source data. We apply the same 52 percent to every country, so the income needed is always 1.92 times the diet cost. It is a global convention, not a country-specific measurement.

The income curve and the slider. Each country’s distribution is rebuilt as a lognormal fitted to its published mean and Gini, because the underlying microdata is not public. Checked against the published unaffordability rates it reaches a correlation of 0.999 and an average error of 1.5 percentage points, with a worst case of 5. Read a percentile as a close estimate, not an exact rank; the fit smooths the real shape of the tails.

Import dependence. Imports divided by domestic supply, computed by us from the Food Balance Sheets.

One year per country. Where a country has several survey years we show the most recent. The median country’s survey is three years old and the oldest is nine, so figures are not all contemporaneous.

Who is missing, and why

129 of roughly 218 economies appear here. A country is included only where diet cost, an income distribution and the context indicators all exist. Food Prices for Nutrition 5.0 withholds PPP diet costs and affordability for Argentina, Myanmar, Somalia, Sudan, Tajikistan and Thailand over unresolved PPP assumptions. Coverage is strongest in Europe, Central Asia and Sub-Saharan Africa and thinnest in East Asia and the Caribbean, so regional medians rest on uneven samples — North America is two countries and South Asia six.
